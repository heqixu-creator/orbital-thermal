Understanding the physics

Model, not a measurement. The differential equations below are the simulator. The numerical values in the controls are illustrative. They are not Google’s satellite, and they are not a qualified thermal design.

1. Convection does not work in a vacuum

A terrestrial heatsink hands heat to moving air. The air carries that energy away and is replaced. On orbit the surrounding gas density is so low that there is no stream to carry heat. Fans, fins, and open racks lose the mechanism they rely on. Conduction still works inside the spacecraft, through boards, straps, heat pipes, and joints. It can move heat to a surface. It cannot, by itself, throw that heat overboard.

2. Thermal radiation is the rejection path

Every surface emits electromagnetic radiation. Net power depends on how well the surface emits, how large it is, and the fourth power of its absolute temperature, minus what it absorbs from whatever it can see. Deep space is an extremely cold sink. In this simplified background that sink is 3 K, so a warm radiator has a large net loss. That loss is the heat-rejection path in the model.

3. The Stefan–Boltzmann law

A gray surface with emissivity ε and effective area A exchanges heat with a black background at T_space according to

P_radiated = ε σ A (T_radiator⁴ − T_space⁴)
σ = 5.670374419 × 10⁻⁸ W·m⁻²·K⁻⁴
T_space = 3 K

The temperatures in that expression are kelvin. The dashboard converts to Celsius only for display. Using Celsius inside the fourth power is not a small unit mistake: 25 °C fed in as if it were 25 K understates the emission by about four orders of magnitude. σ is the CODATA value. The law itself is established physics. Choosing a single background temperature of 3 K, and folding every view factor into A, are modelling choices.

A is the total effective radiating area, counted once. A panel that truly radiates from both sides is entered as the sum of those sides. Do not also multiply by two.

4. Why area and emissivity matter

At a fixed temperature, radiated power is proportional to both ε and A. A larger or blacker radiator rejects more heat without running hotter, so the same chip load equilibrates colder. Emissivity here is the infrared value that sets emission. Solar absorptance is not a separate input. If the panel absorbs sunlight, albedo, or Earth infrared, add that power to the environmental heat load. Leaving that load at 0 W is the shadowed-radiator idealisation. It is not a claim that every orbit is dark. The solar constant near Earth is about 1361 W/m², so an unshaded surface can absorb far more than these chips produce.

5. Why thermal capacitance only affects the rate

Capacitance C is the energy required to raise a node by one kelvin, in J/K. It multiplies the time derivative, so it sets how quickly temperature moves. It cancels out of the steady state. Two radiators with the same area, emissivity, and heat load reach the same final temperature. The heavier one takes longer. That is why the equilibrium prediction on the diagram does not change when you drag the capacitance sliders, while the chart clearly does.

6. Why throttling is necessary

If the chips dissipate more than the radiator can reject at the allowed temperature, energy accumulates and the temperature climbs through the limit. There is no airflow to provide an extra term. The controller is the bound. Across the 2 °C below the configured maximum, the commanded workload is multiplied by a factor that falls from 1 to 0. At the limit the chip heat is fully cut. The “throttling” indication stays on for another 0.5 °C of cooling so the label does not flicker as the node sits on the boundary. The heat load itself has no jump. This is a transparent proportional law for the study, not a flight computer. If environmental heat alone holds the chips above the limit, the controller goes to zero workload and the display says the limit is still exceeded. It does not pretend the radiator can do something it cannot.

Established physics used here

Conservation of energy on each lumped node. Conduction written as Q = G ΔT. The Stefan–Boltzmann law for net emission from a gray surface to a black background.

Assumptions of this model

Two isothermal nodes stand in for the chips and the radiator. Every watt the chips draw becomes heat. The radiator is shielded from direct sunlight. Environmental heating is the number you type, not an orbit. View factors are already folded into A. The sink is a single temperature. There is no survival heater and no eclipse clock. The throttle is the band above, not a flight algorithm.

How the equations are integrated

The pair of ordinary differential equations is advanced with the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method. The step is shortened so that a substep changes a node by about 0.3 K, and so that it stays under about a fifth of the local thermal time constant. The throttle factor is held constant during a step and updated between steps. Temperatures are kept above 0 K. A non-physical trial step is rejected and retried at half the size rather than being allowed to produce NaN or a negative absolute temperature.
